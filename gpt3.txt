TITLE: Language Models are Few-Shot Learners
AUTHORS: Tom B. Brown, Benjamin Mann, Nick Ryder, Melanie Subbiah, Jared Kaplan, and many others (OpenAI)

SECTION: Abstract
Recent work has demonstrated substantial gains on many NLP tasks and benchmarks by pre-training on a large corpus of text followed by fine-tuning on a specific task. While typically task-agnostic in architecture, this method still requires task-specific fine-tuning datasets of thousands or tens of thousands of examples. By contrast, humans can generally perform a new language task from only a few examples or from simple instructions - something which current NLP systems still largely struggle to do. Here we show that scaling up language models greatly improves task-agnostic, few-shot performance, sometimes even reaching competitiveness with prior state-of-the-art fine-tuning approaches. Specifically, we train GPT-3, an autoregressive language model with 175 billion parameters, 10x more than any previous non-sparse language model, and test its performance in the few-shot setting. For all tasks, GPT-3 is applied without any gradient updates or fine-tuning, with tasks and few-shot demonstrations specified purely via text interaction with the model. GPT-3 achieves strong performance on many NLP datasets, including translation, question-answering, and cloze tasks, as well as several tasks that require on-the-fly reasoning or domain adaptation, such as unscrambling words, using a novel word in a sentence, or performing 3-digit arithmetic. At the same time, we also identify some datasets where GPT-3's few-shot learning still struggles, as well as some datasets where GPT-3 faces methodological issues related to training on large web corpora. Finally, we find that GPT-3 can generate samples of news articles which human evaluators have difficulty distinguishing from articles written by humans.

SECTION: 1 Introduction
Recent years have featured a trend towards pre-trained language representations in NLP systems, applied in increasingly flexible and task-agnostic ways for downstream transfer: first single-layer word vector representations, then RNNs with multiple layers of representations, and more recently pre-trained recurrent or transformer language models that are directly fine-tuned, entirely removing the need for task-specific architectures. This last paradigm has led to substantial progress on many challenging NLP tasks, but a major limitation is that while the architecture is task-agnostic, there is still a need for task-specific datasets and task-specific fine-tuning: strong performance typically requires fine-tuning on a dataset of thousands to hundreds of thousands of examples specific to that task. This is undesirable because it limits applicability wherever large labeled datasets are hard to collect, because narrow fine-tuning distributions can encourage the model to exploit spurious correlations, and because humans do not require large supervised datasets to learn most language tasks - a brief natural-language directive or a tiny number of demonstrations is often enough for a human to perform a new task competently.
One potential route towards addressing these issues is meta-learning - in the context of language models, this means the model develops a broad set of skills and pattern recognition abilities at training time, and then uses those abilities at inference time to rapidly adapt to or recognize the desired task. Recent work attempts to do this via what the authors call "in-context learning", using the text input of a pretrained language model as a form of task specification: the model is conditioned on a natural language instruction and/or a few demonstrations of the task and is then expected to complete further instances of the task simply by predicting what comes next. The paper uses the term "meta-learning" to capture the inner-loop/outer-loop structure of the general method, and "in-context learning" to refer to the inner loop of meta-learning, further specialized into "zero-shot", "one-shot", or "few-shot" depending on how many demonstrations are provided at inference time.
Another relevant trend is that transformer language model capacity has increased rapidly (from 100 million to over 17 billion parameters in successive works), with each increase bringing improvements in text synthesis and/or downstream NLP tasks; since in-context learning involves absorbing many skills and tasks within the parameters of the model, it is plausible that in-context learning abilities might show similarly strong gains with scale. This paper tests that hypothesis by training a 175 billion parameter autoregressive language model, called GPT-3, and measuring its in-context learning abilities on over two dozen NLP datasets as well as several novel tasks designed to test rapid adaptation. For each task, GPT-3 is evaluated under three conditions: few-shot learning (as many demonstrations as fit in the context window, typically 10 to 100), one-shot learning (only one demonstration), and zero-shot learning (no demonstrations, only a natural language instruction). GPT-3 achieves promising zero-shot and one-shot results and, in the few-shot setting, is sometimes competitive with or even occasionally surpasses fine-tuned state-of-the-art models, for example reaching 81.5/84.0/85.0 F1 on CoQA in the zero/one/few-shot settings, and 64.3%/68.0%/71.2% accuracy on TriviaQA (the few-shot result being state-of-the-art relative to fine-tuned closed-book models). GPT-3 also displays one-shot and few-shot proficiency at tasks designed to test rapid adaptation or on-the-fly reasoning, such as unscrambling words, performing arithmetic, and using novel words in a sentence after seeing them defined only once, and can generate synthetic news articles that human evaluators struggle to distinguish from human-written ones. The paper also studies data contamination (overlap between training data and benchmark test sets) and discusses broader societal impacts including bias, fairness, and misuse.

SECTION: 2 Approach
The basic pre-training approach - model, data, and training - is similar to GPT-2, with straightforward scaling up of model size, dataset size and diversity, and training length; the use of in-context learning is also similar to GPT-2, but this work systematically explores different settings for learning within the context. The paper identifies four points on a spectrum of how much task-specific data different approaches rely on:
- Fine-Tuning (FT): the most common approach in recent years, involving updating the weights of a pre-trained model by training on a supervised dataset specific to the desired task, typically using thousands to hundreds of thousands of labeled examples. Its main advantage is strong benchmark performance; its disadvantages are the need for a new large dataset per task, potential for poor out-of-distribution generalization, and potential to exploit spurious features of the training data. GPT-3 itself is not fine-tuned in this paper, though it could in principle be.
- Few-Shot (FS): the model is given a few demonstrations of the task at inference time as conditioning, but no weight updates are allowed. For a typical dataset, an example has a context and a desired completion; few-shot gives K examples of context and completion, then one final context, with the model expected to produce the completion. K is typically set in the range of 10 to 100, as many examples as can fit in the model's context window (n_ctx = 2048). The main advantage is a major reduction in the need for task-specific data; the main disadvantage is that results have so far been worse than state-of-the-art fine-tuned models, and a small amount of task-specific data is still required.
- One-Shot (1S): the same as few-shot except only one demonstration is allowed, in addition to a natural language description of the task. One-shot is distinguished because it most closely matches how some tasks are communicated to humans (e.g., giving one demonstration on a crowdworking task).
- Zero-Shot (0S): the same as one-shot except no demonstrations are allowed; the model is given only a natural language instruction describing the task. This provides maximum convenience and robustness but is the most challenging setting, since it may even be difficult for humans to understand a task's format without examples.

SECTION: 2.1 Model and Architectures
GPT-3 uses the same model and architecture as GPT-2, including modified initialization, pre-normalization, and reversible tokenization, except that GPT-3 uses alternating dense and locally banded sparse attention patterns in the layers of the transformer, similar to the Sparse Transformer. Eight different model sizes are trained, ranging over three orders of magnitude from 125 million to 175 billion parameters (the largest being "GPT-3"), to study how ML performance depends on model size; prior work suggests validation loss should scale as an approximately smooth power law with size. The model is partitioned across GPUs along both the depth and width dimensions to minimize data-transfer between nodes.

SECTION: 2.2 Training Dataset
GPT-3 is trained primarily on a filtered version of Common Crawl (constituting nearly a trillion words unfiltered), improved via three steps: (1) filtering a version of Common Crawl based on similarity to high-quality reference corpora, (2) fuzzy deduplication at the document level within and across datasets, and (3) adding known high-quality reference corpora (an expanded WebText dataset, two internet-based books corpora Books1 and Books2, and English-language Wikipedia) to augment and diversify Common Crawl. During training, datasets are not sampled in proportion to their size; higher-quality datasets (WebText2, Books1, Books2, Wikipedia) are sampled more frequently (up to 2-3 or 3.4 epochs over the 300B training tokens) than the larger but lower-quality filtered Common Crawl (sampled less than once), accepting a small amount of overfitting in exchange for higher-quality training data.

SECTION: 2.3 Training Process
Larger models typically use a larger batch size but require a smaller learning rate; the gradient noise scale is measured during training to help guide the choice of batch size. To train the larger models without running out of memory, a mixture of model parallelism within each matrix multiply and model parallelism across the layers of the network is used. All models were trained on V100 GPUs on part of a high-bandwidth cluster provided by Microsoft.

SECTION: 2.4 Evaluation
For few-shot learning, each example in the evaluation set is evaluated by randomly drawing K examples from that task's training set as conditioning, delimited by one or two newlines depending on the task. K can range from 0 up to the maximum the context window allows (n_ctx = 2048), typically fitting 10 to 100 examples; when a development set is available, several values of K are tried on it before running the best value on the test set. For multiple-choice tasks, K examples of context plus correct completion are given, followed by one example of context only, and the model's likelihood for each completion option is compared (normalized per-token, or in some cases by the unconditional probability of the completion). For binary classification, options are given semantically meaningful names (e.g. "True"/"False") and treated like multiple choice. For free-form completion tasks, beam search is used with a beam width of 4 and a length penalty of alpha = 0.6, scored using F1, BLEU, or exact match depending on the dataset.

SECTION: 3 Results (Overview)
Language modeling performance follows a power law when making efficient use of training compute, and this trend, established in prior scaling-law work, continues for GPT-3 with only a slight departure after extending it by two more orders of magnitude. The paper evaluates the 8 models (the 175B-parameter GPT-3 and 7 smaller models) on a wide range of datasets grouped into 9 categories: traditional language modeling and cloze/completion tasks; "closed-book" question answering (using only information stored in the model's parameters, with no external retrieval); translation; Winograd-Schema-like tasks; commonsense reasoning; reading comprehension; the SuperGLUE benchmark suite; natural language inference (NLI); and synthetic/qualitative tasks designed to probe in-context learning and on-the-fly reasoning (e.g. arithmetic, word unscrambling, using novel words). All tasks are evaluated in the few-shot, one-shot, and zero-shot settings. A recurring pattern across most tasks is that few-shot performance improves rapidly with model scale, more rapidly than zero-shot performance, suggesting larger models are more proficient in-context (meta-)learners; the gap between zero-, one-, and few-shot performance often grows with model capacity.

SECTION: 5 Limitations
GPT-3 and this analysis of it have a number of limitations. On text synthesis, although overall quality is high, GPT-3 samples still sometimes repeat themselves semantically at the document level, lose coherence over sufficiently long passages, contradict themselves, and occasionally contain non-sequitur sentences. GPT-3 has noticeable difficulty with certain "comparison" tasks such as determining whether two words are used the same way in a sentence (WIC) or whether one sentence implies another (ANLI), as well as with a subset of reading comprehension tasks, doing little better than chance one-shot or few-shot on some of these despite strong performance elsewhere.
Structurally, the paper focuses on autoregressive language models because sampling and computing likelihoods is straightforward with this model class; this means the experiments do not include bidirectional architectures or denoising objectives, which the literature has shown can improve fine-tuning performance, particularly for tasks involving fill-in-the-blank, comparing two pieces of content, or re-reading a long passage before producing a short answer. A large bidirectional model, or bidirectional models that work with few-/zero-shot learning, are suggested as promising future research directions. More fundamentally, scaling up any LM-like model may eventually run into limits of the pretraining objective itself, which weights every token equally without a notion of what is most important to predict, and large pretrained language models are not grounded in other domains of experience such as video or real-world physical interaction. Poor sample efficiency during pre-training is another shared limitation - GPT-3 still sees far more text during pre-training than a human sees in a lifetime.
A further limitation, or at least an open question, is ambiguity about whether few-shot learning in GPT-3 actually learns new tasks "from scratch" at inference time or simply recognizes and identifies tasks it has learned during training; this may vary from task to task and is an important unexplored research direction. Models at the scale of GPT-3 are also expensive and inconvenient to run inference on, motivating future work on distillation to smaller task-specific models. Finally, GPT-3 shares limitations common to most deep learning systems: its decisions are not easily interpretable, it is not necessarily well-calibrated on novel inputs, and it retains biases present in its training data, which is of particular concern from a societal perspective and is discussed further under Broader Impacts.

SECTION: 6 Broader Impacts (Summary)
Language models like GPT-3 have a wide range of beneficial applications, but also raise concerns about misuse. The paper discusses potential misuse of language models (e.g. for generating misleading news articles, spam, phishing content, or impersonating others), issues of fairness, bias, and representation (e.g. gender, race, and religion biases reflected in generated text due to biases in internet training data), and the energy usage / environmental cost of training and using very large language models.

SECTION: 8 Conclusion
This paper presented a 175 billion parameter language model, GPT-3, which shows strong performance on many NLP tasks and benchmarks in the zero-shot, one-shot, and few-shot settings, in some cases nearly matching the performance of state-of-the-art fine-tuned systems, as well as generating high-quality samples and strong qualitative performance at tasks defined on-the-fly. The paper documents roughly predictable trends of scaling in performance without using fine-tuning, and discusses the social impacts of this class of model. Despite many limitations and weaknesses, these results suggest that very large language models may be an important ingredient in the development of adaptable, general language systems.
